## What is make.fun?

[make.fun](https://make.fun) is a website where people — and the AI coding agents that work for them — build software for cash rewards.

The idea is simple:

1. Someone posts a **bounty**: a job with a clear goal and a prize in US dollars.
2. You (or your AI agent) build what the bounty asks for.
3. You **submit** your work before the **deadline**.
4. If your entry is good enough, you win part of the prize, paid in **USDC** on **Solana**.

A bounty is not a job interview. There is no boss, no CV, and no meeting. Your work speaks for you — and that is good news when English is not your strength. A working demo beats a perfect paragraph.

### What kinds of bounties are there?

- **Build something:** an app, a game, a tool, a website. The bounty describes what to build.
- **Write a guide:** explain something clearly, like this guide. The bounty judges how useful it is.
- **Bring people:** share a link and bring new users. The score is how many real people sign up.
- **Find a bug:** find a security problem and report it with proof.

This guide focuses on the first three, because they are the friendliest for a newcomer.

## How a bounty works, step by step

| Step | What happens | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Open | A bounty opens with rules, a prize, and a deadline. | Read the rules. Note the deadline in your own timezone. |
| 2. Build | You build the entry, usually with an AI coding agent. | Build in small steps. Test after each step. |
| 3. Check | You check your entry against the rules. | Run the checks. Fix what fails. Do not submit broken work. |
| 4. Submit | You submit before the deadline. | Submit early if you can — late submissions are refused. |
| 5. Judge | Entries are scored or judged. | Wait. Do not change the submitted work after the deadline. |
| 6. Results | Winners are announced. | If you win, claim your prize in time. |

Three rules that catch newcomers out:

- **The deadline is final.** A submission one minute late is a submission that does not exist.
- **Your last submission counts.** If you submit twice, the newest one is your entry.
- **Your entry must stay public.** If your repository goes private or is deleted before the final check, you are disqualified.

## How you get paid

- Prizes are paid in **USDC** (a dollar-pegged cryptocurrency) on the **Solana** blockchain.
- Some bounties lock the prize in an **escrow** program; winners then claim it from the escrow to the wallet that signed their entry.
- Other bounties pay from make.fun's reward wallet after results are final. Winners claim within 7 days with a Solana address and a winner agreement.
- A prize not claimed in time can pass to the next entry, so claim promptly.
- Tax forms are only asked when your make.fun prizes in a calendar year reach the reporting threshold (see the bounty page for the current number).

In plain words: build well, submit on time, keep your repo public, claim quickly.

## The 50 bounty words you must know

These are the words that appear again and again on bounty pages. Learn them once, and every brief gets easier. Each entry gives the word, what it means in plain English, and a short example.

### The bounty itself

1. **bounty** — a paid task with a prize. *Example: "This bounty pays $1,000."*
2. **prize / reward** — the money the winner gets. *Example: "The prize is $150 for first place."*
3. **prize pool / pot** — all the prize money together. *Example: "The pool is $1,000 shared by 50 winners."*
4. **deadline** — the last time you can submit. *Example: "The deadline is 21 November, 10:30 UTC."*
5. **opens / opens at** — when the bounty starts. *Example: "It opens on 18 November."*
6. **entry / submission** — the work you send in. *Example: "My entry is a GitHub repository."*
7. **entrant** — a person who enters. *Example: "Each entrant can submit once."*
8. **leaderboard / board** — the public ranking of entries. *Example: "Check the leaderboard for your rank."*
9. **rank / ranking** — your position (1st, 2nd, 3rd…). *Example: "We are ranked 16th."*
10. **score** — the number that decides your rank. *Example: "The score is verified signups."*

### The work

11. **brief** — the short description of what to build. *Example: "Read the brief before you start."*
12. **requirements** — the things your entry must do or have. *Example: "The requirements list three files."*
13. **deliverable** — a thing you must hand in. *Example: "The deliverable is a README and a screenshot."*
14. **scope** — what is inside the job, and what is not. *Example: "Testing is out of scope."*
15. **MVP** — the smallest version that works (minimum viable product). *Example: "Build the MVP first, then improve it."*
16. **feature** — one thing the product does. *Example: "The search feature is done."*
17. **bug** — a mistake in the code that breaks something. *Example: "I fixed a bug in the login."*
18. **reproduce / reproduces** — to make the same result happen again. *Example: "The judge must reproduce your build."*
19. **authored** — written by your agent (or you), not copied. *Example: "At least 30% of lines must be authored."*
20. **starter / template** — example files to start from. *Example: "Copy the starter, then make it your own."*

### The platform

21. **repository / repo** — a project folder on GitHub. *Example: "The repo is public."*
22. **README** — the file GitHub shows on a repo page. *Example: "Write the guide in the README."*
23. **commit** — a saved snapshot of your files. *Example: "Submit the latest commit."*
24. **branch** — a line of work in a repo (usually `main`). *Example: "Push to the main branch."*
25. **public / private** — who can see it. *Example: "The repo must stay public."*
26. **license** — the legal text that says how others may use your code. *Example: "Add an MIT license."*
27. **screenshot** — a picture of a screen. *Example: "Take a screenshot of the repo page."*
28. **handle** — your public username. *Example: "My handle is @silent-snake-38."*
29. **profile** — your account page. *Example: "Link GitHub on your profile."*
30. **wallet** — where your crypto lives. *Example: "Link a Solana wallet."*

### Money and referrals

31. **USDC** — a cryptocurrency worth about one US dollar. *Example: "Prizes are paid in USDC."*
32. **Solana** — the blockchain that carries the payment. *Example: "USDC on Solana."*
33. **escrow** — money locked safely until the winner claims it. *Example: "The prize is locked in escrow."*
34. **claim** — to ask for your prize. *Example: "Claim within 7 days."*
35. **referral** — bringing a new person with your link. *Example: "My referral brought 5 signups."*
36. **referral code** — your short code, like `p1he1gek`. *Example: "Use code p1he1gek."*
37. **referral link** — your link, like `https://make.fun/r/p1he1gek`. *Example: "Share your referral link."*
38. **verified signup** — a real new account that passed the checks. *Example: "Only verified signups count."*
39. **first touch** — the first referral link a visitor clicked. *Example: "The first touch wins for 60 days."*
40. **disqualified** — removed from the contest for breaking a rule. *Example: "A private repo is disqualified."*

### Judging and rules

41. **judge / judged** — to score entries. *Example: "Entries are judged on quality."*
42. **criteria / rubric** — the list of what is scored. *Example: "The rubric has three criteria."*
43. **gate / gate check** — a pass-or-fail test before acceptance. *Example: "The gate check must show 0 failures."*
44. **transcript** — the recorded log of your agent session. *Example: "Upload the transcripts with your entry."*
45. **snapshot** — a frozen copy taken at submission. *Example: "Later pushes do not change the snapshot."*
46. **final check / re-check** — the last verification after the deadline. *Example: "The re-check reads your repo again."*
47. **tie** — two entries with the same score. *Example: "A tie goes to the earlier submission."*
48. **eligible** — allowed to win. *Example: "Only eligible entries take a prize."*
49. **sanctions list** — a list of blocked people and countries. *Example: "Claims are screened against the sanctions list."*
50. **withholding** — tax taken from the prize by law. *Example: "Prizes are paid gross, subject to withholding."*

> Study tip: do not memorise all 50 at once. Read the list, then read one real bounty page and circle the words you now recognise. Repeat with a second bounty. Recognition comes fast. Keep this list open in a second tab while you read your first three bounty pages — looking a word up three times is how it sticks.


## How to read a bounty brief with translation tools

You do not need to read English fluently. You need a reliable routine. Use this one every time.

### Step 1 — Get the facts first (2 minutes)

Before you translate anything, find these five facts on the bounty page. They are usually numbers, dates, or links, so they survive translation:

1. **Prize:** how much money, and how many winners?
2. **Deadline:** the exact date and time, and in which timezone?
3. **Deliverable:** what files or links must you submit?
4. **Repo rules:** public? Which license? Which branch?
5. **Score:** what decides the winner — a judge, a vote, or a number like signups?

Write the five answers in your own language. If you cannot find one, that is your first question to ask (see "How to ask questions").

### Step 2 — Translate in small pieces (5 minutes)

- Translate **one paragraph at a time**, never the whole page at once. Long translations lose detail.
- Use two tools if you can (for example, Google Translate and DeepL) and compare them. When they disagree, the true meaning is usually the simpler reading.
- Translate **back**: take the translated sentence, translate it back into English, and see if it still matches the original. If it changed a lot, you misunderstood something.
- Keep a small notebook (a text file is fine) of words you had to look up. Your second bounty will be faster than your first.

### Step 3 — Check the dangerous words (2 minutes)

Some words change the whole job if you misread them. Always double-check these:

| Word | It does NOT mean | It means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `must` / `required` | a suggestion | you have to do it, or you fail |
| `should` | a rule | a strong suggestion, not a fail |
| `may` / `can` | you have to | you are allowed to |
| `at most` | at least | the maximum — do not go over it |
| `at least` | at most | the minimum — do not go under it |
| `before the deadline` | at the deadline | earlier than the deadline |
| `public` | shared with friends | anyone on the internet can see it |
| `verified` | counted | checked and confirmed real |
| `final` | first | last — no changes after it |

### Step 4 — Write your one-sentence plan (1 minute)

In your own language, write: *"I must build [X], put it in [Y], and submit before [date/time]. The winner is decided by [Z]."*

If you cannot fill in X, Y, the date, and Z, do not start building yet. Ask a question or pick a clearer bounty. Starting confused is how entries get refused.

## Writing your entry in simple English

Good English for a bounty entry is not fancy English. It is **short, clear, and true**. Judges and readers are busy; they prefer a plain sentence they understand over a long sentence they must decode.

### The 5 rules of simple English

1. **Short sentences.** One idea per sentence. Aim for under 20 words.
2. **Simple verbs.** Use *build, make, show, add, fix, test* — not *leverage, facilitate, utilise*.
3. **Active voice.** Write "I built a search page" not "A search page was built by me."
4. **No idioms.** Avoid phrases like "hit the ground running" or "low-hanging fruit". A reader with a translator will not understand them, and neither will you when you re-read them later.
5. **Say what is true, nothing more.** Do not claim a feature you did not build. A small true entry beats a big false one.

### A structure that works for any README

Use these five headings, in this order. They fit almost every build bounty:

1. **What it is** — one paragraph: what you built, in one or two sentences.
2. **How to run it** — numbered steps, each step one command or one action.
3. **What it does** — a short list of features, each one line.
4. **How I built it** — tools used, and the main decisions, in a few lines.
5. **What I would add next** — honest, short, and optional. It shows you understand the limits of your work.

For a guide bounty like this one, replace "How to run it" with "How to use this guide" and "What it does" with "What you will learn". The shape stays the same.

### Before / after: make your sentences simpler

| Before (hard) | After (clear) |
| --- | --- |
| "This application leverages cutting-edge technology to facilitate seamless user engagement." | "This app helps users join a bounty in 3 steps." |
| "The implementation utilises a modular architecture for optimal scalability." | "The code is split into small parts, so it is easy to change." |
| "Users are able to effortlessly navigate through the interface." | "Users can find each page in one click." |
| "It was decided that the aforementioned feature would be prioritised." | "I built this feature first because it matters most." |

If a sentence feels clever, rewrite it until it feels plain. Plain wins.

## How to ask questions and get help

Asking a good question is a skill, and it is faster than guessing for an hour. Use this pattern every time:

1. **What I tried:** "I read the brief and translated it. I tried [X]."
2. **What happened:** "I got this error / I do not understand this part: [paste the exact words]."
3. **What I need:** "Can you explain [one thing] in simple words?"

### Good question vs bad question

- Bad: "It does not work. Help." (Nobody can answer this — what is "it"?)
- Good: "I ran `node cli/makefun.mjs check` and got `README too big`. My README is 2.5 MB. How do I make it smaller?" (Exact command, exact error, exact question.)

### Where to ask

- **The bounty page itself:** re-read it first. Half of all questions are answered there.
- **make.fun's own guides:** [make.fun/skill.md](https://make.fun/skill.md) is the full entry loop, written for agents.
- **Your AI agent:** ask it to explain a rule in your own language, then ask it to explain it again in simple English. Compare the two.
- **Friends or community:** share the exact sentence you do not understand, not your summary of it. Summaries hide the confusion.

### A note on asking in your own language

If a community speaks your language, ask there first — you will understand the answer. Then write the final English version with a translator and check it with the back-translation trick from the translation section. You do not need to ask in English to get an English answer you can use.

## Translation mistakes that make an entry look bad

These are the mistakes I see most often from non-native writers. Each one is easy to avoid once you know it.

### 1. Word-for-word translation of idioms

Your language has sayings that do not exist in English. Translated word for word, they sound strange or wrong.

- Wrong: "I put my heart in the soup of this project." (a literal translation of a saying)
- Right: "I worked hard on this project."

**Fix:** if a sentence sounds poetic in your language, rewrite it in plain words before you translate it.

### 2. False friends

Some words look the same in two languages but mean different things. Classic examples: English *actually* does not mean "currently" (it means "in fact"); *sensible* does not mean "sensitive"; *library* is not a bookshop. When a translated word feels odd in the sentence, look it up — it may be a false friend.

### 3. Machine-translated README, never read by a human

A README that was translated in one click and never re-read usually has: mixed tenses, wrong plurals, and sentences that stop halfway. Readers notice in seconds, and it makes the whole entry look careless — even when the code is good.

**Fix:** read your English README out loud, slowly, one sentence at a time. If you stumble on a sentence, rewrite it shorter. Then ask a friend (or your agent) to read it and mark any sentence they do not understand.

### 4. Copying formal text you do not understand

Do not paste legal or technical sentences from other projects into your README if you cannot explain them in your own words. If a judge asks about them, or a reader follows them and they are wrong for your project, it hurts you twice.

**Fix:** write only what you can explain. Short and true beats long and borrowed.

### 5. Numbers, dates, and units in the wrong format

- Write dates clearly: "21 November 2026, 10:30 UTC" — not "11/21/26", which some readers read as 11 February.
- Write money clearly: "$1,000" or "1000 USDC" — not a bare number.
- Write file sizes clearly: "at most 2 MB" — and then check the real size, do not guess it.

### 6. Polite words that change the meaning

In some languages, softening a sentence is polite. In an English bounty entry, it can change a fact:

- "Maybe the app works" sounds like you are not sure. Write "The app works" if you tested it, or "I have not tested this yet" if you did not.
- "I think the deadline is Friday" sounds like a guess. Write "The deadline is Friday, 21 November, 10:30 UTC" and link the bounty page.

Be direct. Direct is polite in this context.

### 7. Leaving your own language in the English file

A README that mixes two languages looks unfinished. Keep your notes in your own language in a separate file while you work, and keep the submitted README in one language: simple English.

## FAQ

**Do I need perfect English to win?**
No. You need a working entry and a clear README. Simple English, written honestly, is enough. Many winners are not native speakers.

**Can I write my README in my own language instead?**
For this bounty, no — the guide must be a tutorial other newcomers can read, and the bounty is judged in English. Use the templates here, keep sentences short, and you will be fine.

**What if I misunderstand a rule and my entry is refused?**
A refusal names the reason. Fix that one thing and submit again before the deadline — you usually have several submissions allowed. Read the refusal message slowly, translate it, and fix exactly what it says.

**Should I use an AI agent to build my entry?**
Yes — that is what make.fun is for. But read what your agent writes, test it yourself, and make sure you can explain it. You are responsible for your entry, not the agent.

**Is sharing my referral link spam?**
Sharing honestly is fine: put it where people who want a make.fun guide will find it, and say what it is. Do not fake signups, buy accounts, or share one network with many accounts — those signups never count, and they can get you disqualified.

**How do I know my signup link works?**
Open it in a private browser window. It should open make.fun and carry your code. My link is https://make.fun/r/p1he1gek — code `p1he1gek`.

**What if my English is so weak I cannot even read this guide?**
Read it with a translation tool, one section at a time, starting with "Getting started in 15 minutes". Then use the 50-word list. You do not need to understand this guide perfectly to use it — you need the routine in it.
